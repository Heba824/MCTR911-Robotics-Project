# Oral defense notes

1. **How did we derive the mass matrix?** Each body's kinetic energy is the sum of COM translation and rotation about its COM. Substitute `v = Jv @ dq` and `ω = Jω @ dq`. The Hessian in joint velocity gives the sum of `mass * Jv.T @ Jv` and `Jω.T @ I_world @ Jω`, plus reflected armature.
2. **How did we validate it?** Across 200 seeded, legal states with varying velocities and accelerations, we compared mass with `mj_fullM`, bias with `qfrc_bias`, and torque with `mj_inverse`. We reconciled passive effects and excluded contact and constraint effects. The analytical evaluation uses its own Jacobians, energies and inertial forces.
3. **What changes when fuzzy control is enabled?** Model-only computes feedforward acceleration and compensates dynamics. Joint FLC adds an error-driven correction in N·m, which reduces lag and residual error.
4. **Why seven joint-space channels?** The robot has seven independently commanded arm torques, and each joint has a different error, range and sensitivity. Decoupled fuzzy corrections keep the rule table manageable. The dynamics still model mechanical coupling.
5. **How do the five labels and 25 rules work?** Position and velocity errors are scaled, then mapped into LN, MN, S, MP and LP. Four interior intervals provide overlapping membership. The outer sets act as shoulders. Each position/velocity pair selects one of 25 consequents. Min computes activation and clips the output set. Max combines contributions.
6. **Why centroid?** The centroid uses the full aggregate output area and gives intermediate corrections where rules overlap. It is a weighted average of a bounded output domain.
7. **How does Team 1 differ?** It applies three fuzzy channels to XYZ errors and returns task-velocity corrections. Jacobian mapping and a bounded joint velocity servo connect those outputs to the same dynamics and torque bridge.
8. **Why velocity outputs?** The geometric Jacobian maps instantaneous joint velocities to hand velocity. A velocity command gives a direct, unit-consistent input to the inverse kinematics at each control tick.
9. **Why a pseudoinverse?** XYZ imposes three constraints on seven joint velocities. The pseudoinverse gives a minimum-norm mapping when the task is feasible, and the remaining directions permit a posture objective.
10. **What happens near singularity?** A small singular value means very little hand motion along one task direction for a given joint motion. Inverting it would demand very large joint velocities.
11. **How does DLS help?** It replaces `1/σ` with `σ/(σ²+λ²)`. Weak directions are attenuated and the joint-rate request remains bounded with positive damping.
12. **What accuracy do we sacrifice?** The achieved task velocity falls short of the request along weak directions. At the chosen pose, the damped 0.08 m/s request left a 0.07995 m/s residual. This is a deliberate loss of authority near singularity.
13. **How are the limits handled?** The reference passes joint-limit checks. Null-space bias repels near limits, the mapped speed brakes outward motion near each limit, and task speed and commanded accelerations are capped. Controller and bridge enforce the ±87/±12 N·m arm bounds.
14. **Why retain a watchdog?** Without new commands, continuing a held torque could move the arm away from its intended state. The bridge latches after 100 ms, clears arm torque and pauses the simulation. Fresh-state checks also stop the controller from computing or sending commands from expired feedback.
15. **Why did GUI and headless timing initially differ?** The old GUI path shared an executor with physics and command callbacks. A saved trace showed about 295 ms between DDS receipt and bridge callback in one GUI case. The revised path separates rendering and uses a locked state snapshot. Other scheduler or host delays can still occur.
16. **What do the plots demonstrate?** Three matched pipe runs show how feedback improves tracking and what torque it uses. Cartesian telemetry shows weak-direction damping stayed inactive along the nominal path. The offline experiment shows the separate singularity trade-off. Live ROS tables and the GUI timing plot identify their own conditions.
17. **Why did joint FLC win on this trajectory?** The reference specifies every joint directly. Joint FLC corrects those seven errors directly. Cartesian FLC optimizes three task coordinates and spends redundant motion on a secondary posture objective. Its bounded mapping and gains trade off joint and task errors differently.
18. **What are the limitations?** The trajectory is one legal nominal motion in simulation. We did not exercise a physical arm, arbitrary payload or contact, or execute the near-singular command. The GUI still has deadline misses and a reported late safety event. Successful finite trials do not guarantee hard real-time scheduling.
